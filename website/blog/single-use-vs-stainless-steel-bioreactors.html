Single-use vs stainless steel bioreactors: how to choose

Single-use has taken over much of clinical manufacturing, but stainless steel still runs the largest and most demanding processes. Here is how cost, scale, heat and oxygen transfer, validation and carbon data should guide you.

Key takeaways

  • Single-use bioreactors suit multiproduct clinical supply, fast capacity additions and mammalian cell culture up to about 2,000 L; stainless steel (and glass at the bench) remains stronger for high-density microbial fermentation, very large volumes and long-running products.
  • Published cost models disagree, so model the crossover for your own annual demand, utilization and number of products instead of relying on a headline savings figure.
  • Early life cycle assessments favored single-use, but a 2026 study at Roche sites found steel lower in carbon for the operations it compared, under low-carbon energy and updated plastic data, and its authors call for a new, complete assessment.
  • Single-use shifts validation toward suppliers, leachables and bag integrity, and USP <665> now covers plastic parts in reusable equipment too.

Few equipment decisions shape a biomanufacturing plant as much as the choice of single-use vs stainless steel bioreactor. Single-use has taken much of the clinical market: in BioPlan Associates' survey for 2023, single-use equipment was used in 64.0% of upstream clinical production processes, up from 45.0% in 2021.1 Yet most high-volume commercial production still relies on stainless steel, including bioreactors of more than 10,000 L.2

A disclosure: Belach builds reusable glass and stainless-steel bioreactors, not single-use systems, so we have tried to say plainly where single-use is the better choice.

Single-use vs stainless steel bioreactor: the short answer

Single-use bioreactors are usually the better fit for multiproduct clinical supply, for adding capacity quickly and for mammalian cell culture at up to about 2,000 L. Stainless-steel bioreactors, and glass ones at the bench, usually win for high-density microbial fermentation, for volumes well beyond 2,000 L and for long-running, high-demand products, where heat and oxygen transfer, pressure tolerance and steam sterilization pay off. Many plants combine the two.

Cost and total cost of ownership

Capital and time to capacity

A 2001 University College London (UCL) model of an E. coli antibody-fragment process put the capital for a disposables-based plant at under 60% of a conventional one.3 A 2016 review by authors at Natrix Separations, a membrane chromatography company, cites a model in which thirty 2,000 L single-use bioreactors needed $250 million of capital against $352 million for equivalent stainless steel.4 EMD Millipore, another supplier, reported adding single-use capacity in two to three months against 6–12 months for steel,5 and in the UCL model, launching nine months earlier made the two plants' net present values equal.3

Running costs

Here the evidence conflicts. The UCL model found running costs 70% higher for disposables.3 The Natrix review cites one model with 22% lower operating cost per gram for single-use and another with 51% higher consumables costs, and notes that many manufacturers share cost data only at conferences.4 Treat any single savings figure as a scenario, not a fact. Even John Puglia, who directs single-use R&D at Thermo Fisher Scientific, says: Though stainless-steel has higher upfront cost, if you have a blockbuster drug, it could provide more long-term savings because of its durability.6

Where the crossover sits

  • Annual demand. A 2021 UCL model found that single-use continuous plants cut cost of goods by about 35% against stainless-steel batch plants at 100–500 kg a year, but differed by only about ±10% at 1–3 metric tons (the comparison also changes the process mode).7
  • Utilization. A steel plant is built for peak capacity,6 which is wasted if demand falls short,4 while high, steady demand spreads its cost over many batches.6
  • Number of products. Each changeover in steel means cleaning and downtime. ABEC, which sells both technologies, sees multiproduct facilities, smaller quantities and higher titers as favoring single-use, though not absolutely.8

For existing plants, Lorenz Hasler of KBI Biopharma notes: With the cost of construction, commissioning, and automation, it often doesn’t make financial sense to convert an entire facility.6

Changeover, validation and supply

Single-use moves validation work rather than removing it. At EMD Millipore's single-use facility in France, changeover of a 200 L seed bioreactor fell from more than 24 hours in stainless steel to under three hours, with no carryover.5 A steel bioreactor needs validated cleaning, as the FDA has long expected,9 and steam-in-place sterilization that EU GMP Annex 1 expects to be validated and monitored for temperature, pressure and time.10

Supplier qualification and bag integrity

Single-use shifts part of the sterility assurance to the supplier. Annex 1 calls supplier assessment critical, wants evidence of sterilization checked for every unit on receipt, and lists risks specific to single-use, including leachables, leaks and particles. Its second item reads:

The fragile nature of the system compared with fixed reusable systems.

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022)10

A 2026 study based on Roche sites counted defects such as perforated film and broken stirrer blades in 11% of bioreactor bags and 2% of mixing bags over three years.11 The bags were discarded before use, so this is not a batch-failure rate, and the authors stress that the rates are specific to those sites. Budget for spares and incoming inspection anyway.

Supply risk

In 2021, ABEC reported that other suppliers' lead times for single-use bags and tube sets had reached 10–12 months,12 and contract manufacturers overordered equipment and consumables to protect themselves.1 Switching bag supplier is not quick either: under USP <665>, a new component needs its own assessment unless equivalence to a qualified one can be shown.13

Extractables and leachables

Plastics can release compounds into the process stream. A well-documented case is bDtBPP, formed from the polyethylene antioxidant Irgafos 168, which harmed several mammalian cell lines at well below one part per million; experiments suggest that irradiation is important for its formation.14 Single-use bags are presterilized by irradiation, at 25–40 kGy in one supplier's data sheet.15

Yet almost all process equipment contains some polymer that can touch the product.13 USP <665>, official since 1 May 2026, covers both single-use systems, such as bioreactor bags and tubing, and reusable plastic parts such as the O-rings, gaskets and filters of a steel bioreactor; rubber elastomers fall under USP <381>. It is risk-based, sets requirements for products made or sold in the United States, and makes the drug manufacturer, not the equipment supplier, responsible.13 Confirm the implications with your own QA function.

Scale, oxygen and heat: microbial fermentation vs cell culture

In 2019, most suppliers stopped at 2,000 L when ABEC launched a single-use bioreactor with working volumes up to 6,000 L; liquid weight and pressure on the bag have traditionally set the limit.8 Industrial microbial fermenters, by contrast, range from 20,000 to 2,000,000 L.16

Oxygen transfer

The claim that single-use cannot do microbial work is out of date, but a standard cell-culture bag cannot. In a 2015 vendor article, Thermo Fisher wrote that the kLa of single-use cell-culture bioreactors invariably falls below 20 h⁻¹, while steam-in-place fermenters can exceed 400 h⁻¹, and reported over 600 h⁻¹ for its own single-use fermenter.17 Researchers at Sartorius Stedim Biotech named the problem:

The main limitations are a relatively low oxygen transfer rate and cooling capacity.

Dreher et al., Advances in Biochemical Engineering/Biotechnology (2014)18

Even so, by staying within a control space derived from engineering measurements, they reached 60.8 g/L dry cell weight with E. coli and 381 g/L wet cell weight with Pichia pastoris, comparable to stainless steel.18

Check vendor figures independently. A 2021 study at Ipsen measured a maximum kLa of 47 h⁻¹ in a small single-use vessel for which its manufacturer reported 2,500 h⁻¹, a 53-fold gap the authors attribute to the sulfite method behind the vendor figure. Their advice: measure kLa in your own vessels, whoever made them.19

Heat, pressure and sterilization

Amgen engineers estimated that a microbial culture taking up about 300 mmol/L/h of oxygen generates about 150 kJ/kg/h of heat,20 roughly 42 W/kg (our calculation). One supplier's 500 L single-use bioreactor for cell culture is specified for a nominal heating and cooling load of 5,000 W, about 10 W/L (our calculation), with a maximum bag pressure of 0.03 bar and operation up to 40 °C.15 Steel fermenters can raise headspace pressure to improve oxygen transfer and sterilize media in place, although their cooling surface per unit volume shrinks as they grow.20 Our article on bioreactor scale-up explains why.

Purpose-built single-use fermenters, such as the 30 L and 300 L units Thermo Fisher launched in 2014, add water jackets, high-flow exhaust and more agitation; the supplier reported cell densities typical of steel fermenters.17 A 2025 review lists single-use fermenters at 50–1,000 L,2 far below industrial scale.

Environmental footprint: the evidence is unsettled

Single-use was long seen as the greener option.

The early life cycle assessments

A 2008 study by Sinclair and colleagues found that a disposables-based antibody facility used 13,524 L of water per batch against 104,534 L for stainless steel, and emitted 25% less CO2, partly because its single-use plant design was 38% smaller.21 A 2011 assessment by GE Healthcare and BioPharm Services, done to ISO 14040/14044 and critically reviewed, found 34% lower global warming potential and 32% lower energy demand for a 2,000 L single-use antibody process train. It assumed an average US grid and water for injection made mainly from fuel oil and natural gas, and used GE's own equipment; an EU grid did not change the ranking.22

The 2026 re-evaluations

Reiners and colleagues took apart and weighed single-use consumables to quantify the plastic in a 2,000 L batch at two Roche sites. A commercial process in an almost fully single-use facility used 822 kg of consumables plus 128 kg of packaging per batch, about 6.5 t CO2e; a clinical process in a hybrid facility used 360 kg plus packaging, about 2.9 t.11 These figures cover consumables only, not the steam and water of the hybrid's steel equipment. The authors, who abbreviate single-use technology as SUT, conclude that

the CO2 footprint of SUT is significantly higher than previously assumed

Reiners et al., Biotechnology and Bioengineering (2026)11

For a 2,000 L bioreactor run and a 1,200 L buffer preparation done both ways at the hybrid site, the steel versions could emit less than half the CO2 of the single-use ones, with SIP steam 87% of the steel bioreactor's footprint. The key assumptions:11

  • Energy used the site's market-based emission factors, reflecting substantial renewable purchasing (77 g CO2e/kWh cited for Penzberg); steel stayed ahead even with fossil-gas steam.
  • Plastic emission factors were updated; commodity plastic footprints are estimated to be about 30% higher.
  • HVAC and process energy were excluded as equal; the authors argue that commercial-scale single-use plants are not inherently smaller, given consumable warehousing.
  • Defects were counted for 11% of bioreactor bags, and only two operations were compared; the authors call for a new, complete life cycle assessment.

A 2026 laboratory-scale study using data from a GSK facility in Italy found the opposite: 8.7 kg CO2e per gram of protein for a 40 L single-use bioreactor against 14.9 kg for a 20 L steel unit, on the Italian grid. The authors attribute the gap mainly to the larger single-use volume, which halved the number of batches, and call the result specific to that protein and scale.23

Single-use waste is mostly incinerated or landfilled, since mixed plastics and possible biohazard pretreatment limit recycling;4 steel uses water, steam and cleaning chemicals instead.22 Model your own energy mix, volumes and utilization, and ask suppliers for component-level emission data.

Hybrid facilities and how to decide

In a hybrid facility, single-use handles the steps where flexibility pays and steel handles high-volume, low-complexity work. At the Roche hybrid site, media are prepared in existing steel vessels and filtered into the bioreactor, harvest goes to a 2,000 L steel tank, and buffers are made as concentrates in steel and diluted inline from small, mobile bags. For new plants, the authors recommend steel for large-volume buffer and media preparation and at least the option of cleaning and sterilizing in place.11

A decision checklist

  • Demand: annual kilograms per product, how certain the forecast is, and changeovers per year.
  • Peak load: oxygen uptake and heat load against measured, not quoted, kLa and cooling capacity.
  • Volume: the working volume you need, including minimum fill.
  • Conditions: overpressure, media sterilization in place or temperatures above 40 °C.
  • Validation: cleaning and SIP for steel; supplier qualification and incoming inspection for single-use; USP <665> assessments for both.
  • Supply: lead times, second sources, change notification and safety stock.
  • Utilities and waste: steam, water for injection and power, their carbon intensity, and waste routes.

Frequently asked questions

Is a single-use bioreactor cheaper than stainless steel?

It is usually cheaper to buy and faster to install, but consumables raise the cost of each batch, and published models disagree on the total. The crossover depends on annual demand, utilization and the number of products.

Can single-use bioreactors run high-density E. coli or yeast fermentations?

Standard cell-culture bags cannot, because their oxygen transfer, cooling and pressure ratings are too low. Purpose-built single-use fermenters, up to about 1,000 L, have reached high cell densities in supplier studies, but verify kLa and heat removal yourself.

Is single-use more sustainable than stainless steel?

The evidence is not settled. Studies from 2008 to 2011 favored single-use for its water and steam savings. In 2026, a study at Roche sites found steel lower in carbon for the operations it compared, while a laboratory-scale GSK study favored single-use.
