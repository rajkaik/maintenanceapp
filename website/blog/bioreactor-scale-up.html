Bioreactor scale-up: from benchtop to production scale

A process that runs well at 2 L can disappoint at 2,000 L. This guide explains what changes as a bioreactor grows, how the classic scale-up criteria trade off against each other, and how to plan a scale-up that holds.

Key takeaways

  • You cannot keep everything constant when a bioreactor grows. Holding power per volume, tip speed, mixing time or kLa constant forces the other parameters to change.
  • Large vessels are limited by transport rather than by the cells: mixing takes tens of seconds to minutes, cooling capacity per kilogram falls, and CO2 builds up in cell culture.
  • The aim is to keep the cells’ environment within proven ranges, not to copy the lab settings. Choose a primary criterion, then check the others against their limits.
  • Characterize every vessel with water tests, and use scale-down experiments to see how the cells react to large-scale conditions before the first production run.

Most bioprocesses are developed in a benchtop bioreactor of a few liters and earn their money in vessels hundreds or thousands of times larger. A biologic typically moves from 15–250 mL high-throughput systems through 1–10 L bench-scale reactors to pilot and production bioreactors of 200–5,000 L or more.1 Industrial microbial processes go further, from 0.5–10 L laboratory fermenters to 20,000–2,000,000 L, with scale factors from thousands to millions.2 Bioreactor scale-up is the work of getting the same cell behavior, yield and product quality at every one of those steps.

The stakes are high. For industrial microbial processes, the investment needed to reach manufacturing scale, including pilot work and the construction of the plant, is on the order of US$100 million to $1 billion, and the transition typically takes 3–10 years. In that setting, even a 5–10% shortfall in performance or a delay of 3–12 months substantially reduces the return.2 This article explains what physically changes when a bioreactor grows, how the classic scale-up criteria trade off against each other, and what a robust scale-up plan looks like.

Why bioreactor scale-up is harder than it looks

The intuitive approach is to build a bigger copy of the lab vessel and run it with the same settings. It does not work, and the reason is physics. As a review of industrial practice puts it:

One cannot simply enlarge lab-scale equipment and duplicate lab-scale conditions at large-scale

Crater & Lievense, FEMS Microbiology Letters (2018)2

It helps to separate two kinds of parameters. Scale-independent parameters such as pH, temperature, dissolved oxygen (DO) set point and medium composition can be optimized at small scale and then kept. Scale-dependent parameters, such as agitation speed, gas flow, working volume and vessel geometry, cannot, because they determine the flow, mixing and mass transfer that the cells actually experience.1 A small vessel delivers oxygen, removes heat and distributes feed within seconds, so the culture is limited mainly by its own biology. A large vessel cannot keep up, and transport starts to set the pace:

Scale-up generally involves moving processes being controlled by cell kinetics at laboratory scale to control by transport limitations at larger scales.

Chaudhry, BioProcess International (2024)1

The consequences are measurable. In one published example, the biomass yield of an E. coli process producing β-galactosidase fell by 20% when it was scaled from 3 L to 9,000 L.3 In a study led from KTH Royal Institute of Technology in Stockholm, formate accumulated in 22 m³ E. coli fed-batch cultivations, a sign of oxygen-limited zones that the laboratory process did not have.4

What changes as the bioreactor grows

Geometry and surface area

Scale-up usually starts from geometric similarity: the ratios of liquid height to tank diameter (H/T) and of impeller diameter to tank diameter (D/T) are kept the same. Laboratory bioreactors often have an H/T of about 2:1, large ones anywhere from 2:1 to 4:1, and D/T is generally kept between 1/3 and 1/2.1

Keeping the shape has a side effect. Volume grows with the cube of the vessel size, but surface area only with the square. Make every dimension five times larger and the volume grows 125-fold while the wall and liquid surface grow 25-fold, so each liter of culture has one fifth of the surface it had before (simple geometry, our calculation). Less wall per liter means less cooling area, and less liquid surface per liter means less gas exchange with the headspace.

Mixing time and gradients

In small stirred tanks, mixing times are typically under 5 seconds. In large vessels they range from tens to hundreds of seconds, while cells can respond to a changed environment within seconds.3 Mixing times of around 160 seconds have been reported for 100 m³ fermenters, and in large cell-culture bioreactors, which run at low power input, mixing can take minutes.1

When mixing is slower than the cells’ consumption, gradients form. A cell circulating through a large tank passes zones of high substrate near the feed point, zones of low oxygen and, in cell culture, zones of high pH near the base addition port.1 Each pass can trigger stress responses and by-products that the laboratory process never showed. In the 22 m³ study above, the authors attributed part of the lower biomass yield at large scale to cells repeatedly producing and re-consuming by-products as they moved through such zones.4

Heat removal

Microbial fermentations release a lot of heat, and removing it gets harder with scale. In a published characterization of a 300 L pilot reactor and a 10,000 L fermenter, engineers at Amgen estimated the metabolic heat of a culture with an oxygen uptake rate (OUR) of about 300 mmol/L/h at roughly 150 kJ/kg/h. The heat removal capacity determined in water tests was 555–750 kJ/kg/h at pilot scale but only 200–235 kJ/kg/h in the 10,000 L fermenter.5 Both vessels could handle the process, but the margin shrank from about four to five times the heat load to 1.3–1.6 times (our calculation from the published figures). A more intensive process or warmer cooling water could take the large vessel to its limit.

Animal cell cultures are a different story. A high-titer CHO fed-batch process with more than 2 × 107 cells/mL reached a peak OUR of 3.7 mmol/L/h,6 roughly 80 times lower than the microbial example (our calculation). In cell culture, heat is rarely the limit; gas exchange is.

Oxygen in, CO2 out

Oxygen transfer is described by the volumetric mass transfer coefficient kLa. The oxygen transfer rate equals kLa multiplied by the difference between the saturation concentration and the actual dissolved oxygen concentration, so a higher kLa lets a vessel supply more oxygen at the same DO set point. kLa rises with power input and gas flow and can usually be matched at larger scale, but not always. In one family of single-use bioreactors, a kLa of 18 h⁻¹ that the 10 L model reached at full working volume could not be reached by the 1,000 L model at full working volume with any combination of agitation and aeration.7

Carbon dioxide behaves differently. Henry’s constant for CO2 is almost 24 times that of oxygen, which makes CO2 far more soluble in water.8 In small vessels, much of the CO2 leaves through the liquid surface, but that route shrinks as the surface per liter falls.9 Taller liquid columns also add hydrostatic pressure, which raises dissolved CO2 further.1 Rising gas bubbles become saturated with CO2 in less than 10 seconds and then remove no more. In large stirred tanks, CO2 removal therefore depends mainly on how much gas passes through the vessel, the aeration rate in vvm, and is practically independent of stirrer power.8 Hu, Berdugo and Chalmers summed up the consequence:

Somewhat paradoxically, as one continues to scale-up, CO2 removal becomes more of a concern than oxygen addition

Hu, Berdugo & Chalmers, Cytotechnology (2011)6

The effect can be large. In a published case study of a CHO process scaled to 1,000 L, the large bioreactor ran with more foam and lower air-sparge rates than the 4 L vessel, and dissolved CO2 averaged 179 ± 9 mmHg (about 240 mbar) in the large bioreactor against 68 ± 13 mmHg (about 90 mbar) at 4 L. The large-scale runs often had to be harvested on day 10. Revised sparging strategies, developed in cell-free tests and based on gas throughput, brought dissolved CO2 well below the historical levels, extended the culture from 10 to 14 days and almost doubled the final titer.9 For comparison, inhibition of CHO cells has been reported when pCO2 falls outside 40–100 mbar.10 (The mbar values are our conversion.)

Scale-up criteria compared

All the scale-dependent quantities, from power input to tip speed and circulation time, depend on two variables: the impeller diameter and its rotational speed. Once the geometry is fixed and one criterion is held constant, everything else follows, and most of it changes. The comparison below, adapted in BioProcess International from Lara et al., shows what happens for a 125-fold increase in volume with geometric similarity, which corresponds to five times the diameter (our calculation):1

Criterion held constantWhat happens to the other parametersIn practice
Power per volume (P/V)Impeller speed falls, tip speed rises, kLa increases, circulation and mixing time almost tripleA common choice; check mixing time and CO2 removal
Impeller tip speedP/V falls to one fifth, kLa falls, circulation time increases fivefoldUsed infrequently for cell culture; risk of oxygen limitation
Circulation time (same impeller speed)P/V increases 25-foldUsually infeasible because of the very high power input
Reynolds numberP/V falls to 1/625Not feasible
kLaOxygen supply matches; mixing time, hydromechanical stress and CO2 removal do not necessarily follow8, 11A common choice for aerobic processes; check the other parameters

Constant kLa, constant aeration rate, tip speed and constant P/V are among the most commonly used criteria for cell culture.11 For geometrically similar vessels in turbulent flow, the agitation speed at large scale (N2) follows from the small-scale speed (N1) and the impeller diameters (D1, D2):1

Constant P/V: N2 = N1 × (D1/D2)2/3
Constant tip speed: N2 = N1 × (D1/D2)

No single criterion keeps everything the same, so the practical rule is to choose a primary criterion and then check that the others stay within acceptable limits. The objective, as the BioProcess International series frames it, is not to keep scale-dependent parameters constant but to define operating ranges in which the cells’ physiological state, productivity and product quality are maintained across scales.1

The choice matters even at small scale. In a study that scaled a CHO culture from 96-well plates via shake flasks to a 1.5 L stirred tank, using the maximum oxygen transfer capacity as the scale-up parameter led to excessive hydromechanical stress in the stirred tank, while using volumetric power input reproduced the cultivation and gave the same final antibody titer.11 Criteria can also drift within a single run. In a CHO fed-batch process run at constant agitation, the volume added by feeding lowered P/V by 19% and kLa from 14 to 12 h⁻¹ by the end of the culture.12

Microbial fermentation versus cell culture

The same physics applies to every bioreactor, but the bottlenecks differ. Bacteria and yeasts grow fast, consume oxygen quickly and release a lot of heat, and they tolerate vigorous agitation. Microbial scale-up is therefore usually limited by oxygen transfer, cooling capacity and substrate gradients near the feed point.3, 5

Animal cells grow more slowly and need far less oxygen, but were long considered too fragile for vigorous stirring. The evidence has moved on. In his review of large-scale animal cell culture, Alvin Nienow concluded that ‘shear sensitivity’ due to agitation and bursting bubbles is no longer considered a major problem.13 An analysis of 18 CHO processes run in 55 bioreactors from 250 mL to 5,000 L found a mean Kolmogorov length, the size of the smallest turbulent eddies, of 78 µm. That is much larger than CHO cells at under 20 µm, so turbulence was not expected to damage the cells.14

Damage does still occur, but mainly where bubbles burst at the liquid surface, in the foam layer, and at the sparger when gas leaves the holes very fast: one study found damage to NS0 cells above a gas entrance velocity of 30 m/s. The same review notes that while CHO, insect and hybridoma cells are generally accepted as robust, other cell lines are not as robust.6

Typical operating windows reflect this. In mammalian cell culture, P/V is typically kept between 10 and 80 W/m³, large single-use bioreactors are typically limited to a maximum of 20–30 W/m³, and impeller tip speed is typically kept below 1 m/s in benchtop bioreactors.12 For animal cells, the scale-up constraints are more often mixing time, pH gradients around the base addition and, as shown above, CO2 removal.1, 8

Scale-down models: bringing the large scale to the lab

Production-scale experiments are too expensive to use for development. Delvigne and colleagues note that pilot or production tests often cost €40,000–80,000 per run and illustrate the savings if the usual 10–15 such tests could be reduced to 2–3.15 The alternative is to bring the large scale to the lab. A scale-down model is a small bioreactor set up to reproduce what the cells experience at production scale: either the average conditions, with matched engineering parameters, feeding and control, or deliberately the gradients of the large tank.3, 2

Gradient scale-down models come in several forms:3, 2

  • Two-compartment systems connect a stirred tank to a plug-flow loop that cells pass through without back-mixing, mimicking a trip through a poorly mixed zone. In one CHO study, a 5 L stirred tank was coupled to a 1.07 L plug-flow reactor with mean residence times of 70–120 s. At residence times of 90 s or more, viable cell density in the stationary phase fell by up to 30% compared with a well-mixed tank, and host cell protein impurities increased.
  • Multi-compartment vessels divide one stirred tank with internal disks. In a 42 L example for L-lysine production, this made the mixing time 13 times longer than in a standard vessel of the same size, and sugar consumption fell by 14%.
  • Oscillating control uses the control system to make critical parameters fluctuate: oscillating the agitation rate to mimic kLa differences, blending gases with oscillating flow ratios, or pulsing the substrate feed to mimic concentration gradients.

A scale-down model is only as good as its link to the real process, and regulators expect that link to be understood. The US FDA’s process validation guidance says:

It is important to understand the degree to which models represent the commercial process, including any differences that might exist

US FDA, Process Validation: General Principles and Practices (2011)16

Comparing engineering parameters rather than settings also helps when a process moves between bioreactors of different designs. When three laboratories compared five bioreactor systems with different inner geometries for a human cell line, growth rates agreed best within a common mixing-time window of 8–13 s.17 For more on qualifying small-scale models and running them in parallel, see our article on parallel bioreactors and design of experiments.

A practical scale-up checklist

Crater and Lievense build their advice on three principles: begin with the end in mind, be diligent in the details, and prepare for the unexpected.2 In practice that means:

  • Know the target vessel early. Geometry, impellers, sparger, cooling capacity and maximum gas flow limit what the process can do at scale. Develop the lab process with them in mind, not the other way round.
  • Characterize every vessel. Water tests show what each bioreactor can actually deliver: heat removal, oxygen transfer, evaporation and mixing time. The Amgen engineers who published a test platform for microbial fermenters recommend repeating such tests periodically to confirm that equipment capability has not shifted.5
  • Choose a primary criterion and check the rest. Scale on P/V or kLa, for example, then confirm that tip speed, mixing time, gas flow and CO2 removal stay within acceptable ranges.1
  • Measure what the cells experience. Off-gas analysis, dissolved CO2 and osmolality reveal problems that DO and pH readings alone do not show.
  • Keep sensors, control logic and software the same at every scale. When control loops, alarms and data structures are identical, differences between scales can be traced to the process rather than to the equipment.
  • Test the gradients in a scale-down model before the first large-scale run, and qualify the model against large-scale data as it becomes available.3, 16
  • Do not skip the pilot. Piloting, usually at 1–10% of full manufacturing scale, costs approximately 5–20% of the total project. It can be tempting to skip it, but Crater and Lievense warn that the downside far outweighs the modest upside.2

Frequently asked questions

What is the best criterion for bioreactor scale-up?

There is no single best criterion. Constant power per volume and constant kLa are common starting points, but neither keeps mixing time, hydromechanical stress and CO2 removal constant, so every scale-up needs a check of all of them against what the cells tolerate.

What is kLa?

kLa is the volumetric mass transfer coefficient for oxygen, expressed in h⁻¹. Multiplied by the difference between the saturation and the actual dissolved oxygen concentration, it gives the oxygen transfer rate. It depends on agitation, gas flow, sparger design, working volume and the medium, so it is measured for each vessel.

Why does CO2 build up in large cell-culture bioreactors?

Large vessels have less liquid surface per liter, higher hydrostatic pressure and longer gas residence times. Bubbles saturate with CO2 quickly, so removal depends mainly on the total gas flow, and without enough gas throughput dissolved CO2 rises well above bench-scale levels.

What is a scale-down model?

A laboratory bioreactor set up to reproduce production-scale conditions, either the average conditions or the gradients of the large tank. Once qualified against large-scale data, it can be used to study scale effects, troubleshoot and characterize the process without production-scale runs.
